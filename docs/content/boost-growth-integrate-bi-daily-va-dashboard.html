If bookings live in a calendar, marketing lives in an ad account and client health lives in whoever last spoke to them, you already own the data — you just cannot see it. A question as simple as "how did last month actually go" turns into an afternoon of rebuilding the answer from three tabs and a spreadsheet. That is not a reporting problem. It is a decision-speed problem, and a daily dashboard is what fixes it.

What belongs on the dashboard every morning

Business intelligence, in this context, means one screen that answers three questions before your first call: what came in, what it cost to bring in, and who is about to leave. Everything else is a report. Reports get opened monthly; dashboards get opened daily, and that frequency is the entire value.

  • Bookings — is the funnel converting, and at what speed?
  • Marketing — which channel produces booked work rather than clicks?
  • Customer health — which clients are drifting, and which are ready to grow?

Three tiles, refreshed automatically, read in a fixed order, every morning. Any dashboard that needs a manual export to be true is a project, not a process.

Bookings: read the funnel, not the total

A booking count tells you what happened last week. A funnel tells you where it will go next week. The moment you split enquiries into contacted, qualified and booked, a flat month stops being a mystery and becomes a specific stage with a specific leak.

Enquiry arrivesThe number everyone already tracks — and the one that hides where the loss happens.
Contacted inside the hourThe first stage that can silently fail. A reply the next morning is a different conversation.

Two numbers to put side by side

Conversion rate without velocity is half a picture. A funnel that converts well over eleven days still feels slow to the buyer, and a funnel that converts mediocrely in two days still wins the booking. Track them together or track neither — one alone will tell you to fix the wrong stage.

Marketing: make every dollar traceable

Most campaigns are judged on the platform's own dashboard, where a click is success and a booking is a coincidence. Pull the spend into the same view as the bookings and the argument ends: a channel either produces booked work at a cost you can name, or it produces traffic.

Metric What it actually tells you Act when
Cost per lead What one enquiry costs on a channel It climbs while lead quality stays flat
Cost per booking What one booked job costs after qualification Two channels tie on cost per lead but not on this
Return on ad spend Revenue returned per unit of spend, by campaign Below break-even and still running "for awareness"
Value by source Whether cheap leads stay cheap after onboarding The lowest cost per lead has the shortest client life

The channel that looks cheap and is not

Cost per lead is the easiest metric to win and the easiest one to be misled by. A channel that delivers twice as many leads at half the cost is a loss if those leads book once and never renew. Read cost per lead, then read value by source, then decide. Reallocating on the first number alone is how ad budgets get rebuilt every quarter with nothing to show for it.

Customer health: churn shows up weeks before it happens

Clients rarely cancel without warning. They go quiet, they stop replying inside the day, their task queue grows faster than it drains, and then one Friday there is an email. All of those are visible in the data you already generate — you just have to put them next to a name.

Signal Leading or lagging Read it when
Days since last contact Leading It passes the cadence you promised them
Open task backlog Leading It grows faster than it drains for two weeks
Average response time Leading It doubles — usually a sign the work outgrew the plan
Satisfaction score Lagging It moves, because by then the decision is already made
Renewal date Lagging You find it inside renewal week rather than before it

Leading signals are the whole point. A satisfaction score is a post-mortem; a backlog you can see on a Tuesday is a conversation you can still have.

Not a wall of charts. Three tiles, each with one number, one comparison and one owner. If a tile cannot name the person who acts on it, it is decoration and it should come off the screen.

What usually goes wrong

  • A dashboard nobody owns. Every tile needs a name against it, or it becomes background.
  • Metrics with no definition. "Lead" and "booking" mean different things to sales and to delivery; write the definition down.
  • Manual exports dressed up as automation. If a person has to download a file, the number is already a week old.
  • Tracking everything. Forty metrics is a data dump. Three is a decision.
  • Judging on one number. Conversion alone hides velocity; cost per lead alone hides client lifetime.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a BI tool, or is a spreadsheet enough?

A spreadsheet is enough while one person owns it and the sources are small. It stops being enough the moment the numbers have to be trusted by two people, because a spreadsheet cannot prove where a figure came from. The test is not the tool, it is whether the number refreshes without anyone touching it.

How much of this can a virtual assistant actually run?

The build and the daily read, which is most of it. A VA can define the metrics with you, connect the sources, keep the definitions current and flag a tile that has gone quiet. What a VA should not do is decide what a number means for strategy — that stays with you, and it is why every tile names an owner.

We already have a CRM. Is this a second system?

No. The CRM holds records; the dashboard holds the three answers you need before a call. In practice the dashboard reads the CRM rather than replacing it, and the only new thing it asks of the team is that the CRM stays accurate enough to be read.

What about bilingual operations?

Split lead source and value by language. It is one extra column and it answers a question most agencies cannot: which channel actually reaches the English-speaking buyer and which reaches the Spanish-speaking one, so budget goes where it converts rather than where it is easiest to buy.
